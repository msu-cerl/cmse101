Final Case Design Project

  • Students: Your Case Design team (essay and presentation); individually (cover letter)
  • Framework: Data-Tools-Practices-Actions (DTPA) and the Four Pillars of AI Literacy

You have spent the semester building your case one scaffold at a time. Scaffold 1 scoped the problem; Scaffold 2 built the evidence base and committed to a design direction. The final project is where your team makes the case for that design, first in writing, then in front of the class, and where each of you looks back at what you learned.

The final project has three parts:

Part A: Final Case Design Essay#

What this essay is#

Your final essay is an argument. Its job is to convince a skeptical, well-informed reader that your proposal is the right response to the problem or system you have been studying. That reader has not seen your scaffolds and has no reason to agree with you yet.

This is a change from Scaffolds 1 and 2. They asked you to cover every DTPA component at roughly equal depth so that you would build a complete picture of your case. You have that picture now. The essay asks you to decide which parts of it matter most to your design and to build your argument around them.

The essay does not need to follow the section headings from Part 3 of the template. Organize it around your argument, not the template. Most of the material you wrote for Scaffolds 1 and 2 will still appear, but revised, reordered, and put to work.

What the essay must do#

1. State a clear thesis#

By the end of your introduction, a reader should be able to say in one sentence what you propose and why. For example:

“The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services should require that any Medicare prior-authorization denial flagged by an algorithm be reviewed by a same-specialty clinician before the patient is notified, because the evidence shows that the denials these systems generate are reversed at rates that no accuracy figure would predict, and the patients least able to appeal bear the cost.”

Everything else in the essay should support, explain, or test that sentence.

2. Present the design precisely#

Describe your solution (Pathway I) or restriction (Pathway II) in enough detail that someone could build, implement, or enforce it, and someone who disagrees could argue against it.

  • Pathway I: What does the system do, for whom, and how? What data does it use, what kind of model, who operates it, and what does it decide or recommend? What does it deliberately not do? How would you know in a year whether it is working, and what would make you shut it down?
  • Pathway II: What exactly is restricted, required, or prohibited, and who does it apply to? State the core of the restriction as precisely as a policy would, then explain it in plain language. Who enforces it, how would violations be detected, and what happens then?

3. Foreground the DTPA components that carry your argument#

Every case touches all four components, but in most designs one or two do the heavy lifting. A restriction on predictive policing may stand or fall on Data (arrest records measure police activity, not crime). A tutoring system may stand or fall on Practices (who is in the loop when the model is wrong). A rent-pricing restriction may stand or fall on Actions (what the system rewards landlords for doing).

Your essay should:

  • Name the component(s) most salient to your design and argue for why they are the crux. This is a claim, and it needs evidence like any other.
  • Develop those components in depth, with specific, cited evidence, the way you did in your Full Case Studies.
  • Address the remaining components more briefly, showing that you have considered them and explaining why they matter less to this design (or how your design handles them).

An essay that gives every component 300 words of equal weight has not yet made this decision.

4. Show the AI literacies your work required#

Choose the AI literacies (Data, Quantitative, Ethical, Critical) that are most relevant to your case and make clear how your team used them. Most teams will foreground two; you do not need to address all four.

Don’t just name the literacy; show it in use. The difference looks like this:

Naming (avoid): Our project uses quantitative literacy because we looked at accuracy numbers.

Showing (aim for): The vendor reports 94% accuracy, but that figure was measured on a validation set in which only 3% of cases were positive; a model that flagged no one would score 97%. The number that matters for our design is the false-negative rate among patients who were later hospitalized, which the vendor does not publish. Our proposal requires it.

The second version never uses the words “quantitative literacy,” but it is plainly doing it. You may want to say explicitly which literacies your work draws on (a sentence in your introduction or conclusion is enough), but the evidence of the literacy lives in your analysis.

5. Take the strongest objection seriously#

Identify the most serious objection to your proposal, not the easiest one, and respond to it. What could go wrong? Who might be harmed by your design? What remains uncertain? The strongest essays are honest about their weaknesses; Amsterdam’s welfare algorithm is a reminder that following every best practice does not guarantee a fair outcome.

6. Close with a critically pragmatic view#

Explain why your design neither assumes that technology will solve the problem nor assumes that it can only cause harm. What does your proposal ask of the people and institutions involved, beyond the technology?

Evidence and references#

Your essay should rest on the research you have done all semester.

  • Build from your Scaffold 2 annotated bibliography: at least eight sources, drawing on at least three different kinds (reporting, official record, organization’s own account, research, advocacy, first-hand account).
  • You may (and are encouraged to) cite course readings and the Full Case Studies your team completed when they inform your design.
  • Every factual claim must be traceable to your reference list. Every number must say where it came from, and every accuracy or error figure must say what it was measured on and compared to.
  • Use APA style for in-text citations and the reference list.

A suggested shape#

There is no required structure, but most strong essays make these moves, in roughly this order:

MoveApprox. lengthWhat it does
Opening and thesis~300 wordsA concrete hook (a person, an incident, a number), the problem or system, and your thesis
Why it matters~300 wordsSignificance and stakeholders, including those with little power over the outcome
The crux~1,000 wordsIn-depth analysis of the DTPA component(s) your argument turns on
The rest of the picture~300 wordsThe remaining DTPA components, and why they matter less here or how your design handles them
The proposal~600 wordsYour design, stated precisely, with implementation or enforcement
Objections and risks~350 wordsThe strongest objection, unintended consequences, and mitigations
Conclusion~150 wordsYour critically pragmatic case, in brief

You will notice that AI literacies don’t have their own row. They should run through the whole essay, especially in the crux and the objections.

Format#

  • 3000 words minimum, not counting the reference list, tables, response-to-feedback document, or “Changes after the Showcase” section.
  • Written in connected prose, in one consistent voice. Tables are welcome where they help, but the argument lives in the paragraphs.
  • Include a title, your team members’ names, and your pathway at the top.
  • Disclose any AI use, as you do in your weekly reports: which tool, and what you used it for.

What you submit, and when#

Fri, Dec 4: Draft. A complete draft of the essay, plus a response to Scaffold 2 feedback (>250 words) using the feedback response format.

Tue, Dec 15: Final. The revised essay, with a section at the end titled “Changes after the Showcase” (about 150 words) describing what you revised in response to the class’s feedback and questions, and why. “We considered this and kept our approach” is fine, as long as you explain why. This section is where you provide the evidence of decision-making that the 4.0 Case Design criteria ask for.

Before you submit the essay#

  • Could a reader state your thesis in one sentence after your introduction?
  • Does the essay argue for why your foregrounded DTPA component(s) are the crux of your design?
  • Does it show your chosen AI literacies at work, rather than just naming them?
  • Is your proposal specific enough that someone could implement it, or argue against it?
  • Have you responded to the strongest objection, not the easiest one?
  • Is every factual claim traceable to your APA reference list?
  • Have you disclosed any AI use?

Part B: Class Showcase Presentation#

What the presentation is (and isn’t)#

The presentation is not your essay read aloud or summarized slide by slide. Your essay is written for a reader with time to follow a long argument; your presentation is for a room with twelve minutes. The goal is to make your classmates understand your case, care about it, and remember your proposal.

A sharp presentation makes one argument, clearly. Pick your most compelling evidence, the DTPA component(s) at the heart of your design, and the clearest version of your proposal. Trust the essay to hold the rest.

Format#

  • 12 minutes presenting, then 3 minutes of questions. We will keep time so every team gets its full slot.
  • Everyone on the team speaks. Split the talk so each person has a substantial part, not just an introduction or a closing line. Practice the handoffs.
  • Submit your slides on D2L before class on your presentation day.
  • Three groups present on Dec 7 and Dec 9, and two on Dec 11. Your day will be posted on D2L.

What the presentation must include#

  • Your case and your proposal, stated clearly enough that the audience could repeat them.
  • The DTPA component(s) most salient to your design, and why they matter. You do not need to walk through all four.
  • The AI literacies your case depends on. Help the audience practice them: show them the misleading accuracy figure, the missing group in the dataset, the incentive nobody intended. Don’t just list the pillars on a slide.
  • What could go wrong with your proposal, and what you would do about it.

Suggested structure#

SectionTimeWhat to cover
Hook~1.5 minOne concrete example that makes the audience care: a person, a number, or an incident
The problem or system~1.5 minWhat it is, who is affected, and your strongest one or two pieces of evidence
The crux~3 minThe DTPA component(s) your design turns on, and the literacy it takes to see the problem
The proposal~3.5 minWhat you propose, who implements it, and how it works. This is the heart of the talk.
Risks and trade-offs~1.5 minThe strongest objection to your proposal, and your response
Closing~1 minThe one thing you want the audience to remember

Eight to twelve slides is usually enough. Put a source on every slide that shows a number or a claim.

Presentation guidelines#

  • Rehearse with a timer at least once. Twelve minutes goes quickly.
  • Prepare for questions. Write down the three questions you most expect, and agree on who will answer each.
  • Design for the back of the room. Large text, few words per slide, charts with labeled axes.
  • Engage, don’t recite. A question to the audience, a short demonstration, or a “what would you decide?” moment can do more than another slide of text.

Giving and using class feedback#

After each presentation, audience members will respond to three questions:

  1. What was the most convincing part of this proposal?
  2. What is the biggest weakness or unanswered question?
  3. If you were implementing (or affected by) this proposal, what would you want to know?

Your team will receive this feedback after the Showcase. Use it to revise your essay and write your “Changes after the Showcase” section before Dec 15.

The Showcase is not graded on its own, but completing it is required for a Case Design grade of 3.0 or higher (see the syllabus), and you may not submit your final essay until your group has presented.

Part C: Individual Cover Letter#

What the cover letter is#

Each member of your team writes their own cover letter to accompany the final project. It is an individual submission and is not shared with your teammates. Think of it as the last, and most important, of your weekly Individual Effort & Metacognitive Reports: a look back at the whole semester rather than a single week.

Write it as a letter addressed to Danny, about 500 words, in the first person.

What the cover letter must cover#

1. What you learned from your case design (about 125 words)#

What do you understand now about your case, about AI, or about designing (or restricting) technology that you did not understand when your team started in October? Be specific: name a moment, a source, a piece of feedback, or a decision your team made that changed your thinking.

2. How you have grown in DTPA and AI literacies (about 150 words)#

How has your understanding of the DTPA framework and the Four Pillars of AI Literacy changed since the start of the semester? The strongest letters compare earlier work to later work. For example, put a sentence from your Week 2 case analysis next to a passage from your final essay and explain what you can see now that you couldn’t then.

3. The course grade you have earned, and why (about 225 words)#

State the overall course grade you believe you have earned, and make the case for it with evidence. Use the syllabus grading scheme: Class Engagement (40%), Case Studies (30%), and Case Design (30%), each evaluated at 2.0, 3.0, 3.5, or 4.0, then combined and rounded to the nearest 0.5.

Your evidence should include:

  • Your record. Where you stand against the syllabus thresholds for each component: attendance, exit tickets, forum posts, case analyses, effort reports, and the Case Design milestones. Check D2L.
  • Deeper engagement. Forum replies, leading a class, and how your team responded to Scaffold feedback and Showcase feedback. These are the criteria that separate 3.0 from 3.5 and 4.0.
  • Specific work. Point to or briefly quote particular forum posts, case analyses, effort reports, or passages from the final essay that show the quality of your engagement.
  • Your contribution to your team. What you did on the Case Design: which sections you led or wrote, what research you found, what problems you helped solve.

Show your reasoning: the level you claim for each component and how they combine. An evidence table like the one below does not count toward your word count:

Component (weight)Level I claimEvidence
Class Engagement (40%)
Case Studies (30%)
Case Design (30%)
Overall

I will read your letter alongside the gradebook, your effort reports over the semester, and your team’s final project.

Format#

  • About 500 words, not counting the evidence table.
  • Addressed to Danny, written in the first person, in paragraphs.
  • Disclose any AI use, as in your weekly reports.
  • Due Tue, Dec 15, 11:59pm, as an individual upload on D2L.

Before you submit the cover letter#

  • Does it name something specific you learned from your case design?
  • Does it show growth by comparing earlier work to later work?
  • Does it state an overall course grade and justify each component with evidence?
  • Does it describe your own contribution to the team?
  • Have you disclosed any AI use?
